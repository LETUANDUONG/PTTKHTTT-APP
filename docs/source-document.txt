Abstract
This report specifies a complete e-commerce information system for an stationery online retailer. The proposed system supports customer accounts, product and category management, product browsing and search, shopping carts, checkout, payment, inventory, delivery tracking, reviews, promotions, administration, and management reporting. The report follows the information-system lifecycle from requirements determination through analysis, AI-enabled e-commerce opportunities, design, and code. AI functions are treated as supporting services within a dependable transactional platform, not as a replacement for the core e-commerce processes.
Chapter 1 Requirements Determination
1.1 Business Background
1.1.1 Mart Overview
Hoodoo is a medium-sized online stationery retailer that serves customers through a web storefront and a mobile-responsive customer interface. The business sells pens, pencils, notebooks, paper, school supplies, files, folders, and office accessories. Its value proposition is to provide customers with reliable product information, transparent pricing, convenient ordering, secure payment options, and predictable delivery. The retailer competes through catalogue quality, product availability, service responsiveness, promotions, and the ability to turn a customer visit into a completed and well-supported order.
The company currently operates with information distributed across spreadsheets, staff messages, and separate partner portals. Product records can be edited by several people, inventory changes may be recorded after the physical movement occurs, and staff must often reconcile order, payment, and shipping information manually. This creates a clear need for an integrated e-commerce information system. The proposed system is not merely a website: it is the operational platform that connects customer-facing shopping activities with catalogue administration, inventory control, order processing, payment, fulfilment, delivery, customer service, and management reporting.

This chapter investigates the organisation before prescribing a technical solution. It explains the business context, identifies stakeholders and their information needs, analyses the information managed by the business, examines current problems and their consequences, and defines the purpose, boundary, information flows, and objectives of the proposed system. The findings are used directly in Chapter 2 for functional analysis, in Chapter 3 for AI-enabled opportunities, in Chapter 4 for architecture and data design, and in Chapter 5 for implementation planning.
 
1.1.2 Business Model
Hoodoo operates as a business-to-consumer retailer. Customers explore the online catalogue, compare available products, place selected items in a shopping cart, submit delivery and payment information, and receive an order confirmation. The retailer earns revenue from the difference between selling price and the cost of goods, while promotions may be used to stimulate demand, clear inventory, or encourage repeat purchase. Product availability, price, service quality, and delivery reliability directly influence conversion, repeat purchase, and customer trust.
The business model contains two connected value streams. The first is the customer value stream: product discovery, evaluation, purchase, payment, delivery, and after-sales support. The second is the operational value stream: catalogue maintenance, inventory replenishment, stock allocation, order fulfilment, partner coordination, reporting, and performance improvement. The information system must serve both streams using the same authoritative data rather than allowing each department to maintain incompatible copies.

1.1.3 Products and Customers
The stationery catalogue contains products with unique product identifiers (product_id), names, categories, descriptions, images, prices, supplier references, promotion eligibility, selling units, and stock quantities. Relevant attributes depend on the product category: ink colour and tip size for pens; paper size and weight for paper; and page count and ruling for notebooks. Each separately sold colour, size, or pack configuration is maintained as a separate product record with its own product_id, price, selling unit, and stock quantity. Selling units may include an individual item, box, pack, or ream, and the catalogue must state the quantity contained in each unit. Individual items and packs are stocked as separate products; automatic conversion between loose items and packs is not supported. Cart lines, order lines, stock reservations, and fulfilment identify the selected product by product_id and express quantities in that product's stated selling unit. Repeated additions of the same product_id increase the existing cart-line quantity, while different product identifiers remain separate lines. The system must retain accurate product-level price and availability information and display enough product specifications for customers to select the intended stationery item.

Hoodoo serves individual stationery buyers, including students, teachers, and people purchasing supplies for personal study or office use. Customers may register or, where policy permits, shop as guests. They may arrive through direct search, social campaigns, paid advertisements, email promotions, or repeat visits.

Their needs differ: a first-time visitor needs clear discovery and reassurance; a returning customer needs efficient reordering and trustworthy order history; a customer with a problem needs prompt service and an unambiguous status. The proposed system must support these situations without exposing personal data or allowing one customer to access another customer’s information.

1.2 Stakeholder Analysis

1.2.1 Stakeholder Classification
A stakeholder is a person, role, organisation, or system that affects or is affected by the e-commerce information system. Stakeholders are classified as internal or external so that the investigation distinguishes between the organisation’s own responsibilities and the responsibilities of partners. Not every stakeholder is an actor in the use case model. An actor directly interacts with the system; a stakeholder may instead have an interest in its output, controls, or performance.

Internal stakeholders include the administrator, sales staff, warehouse staff, and manager. They require information and functions that support controlled business operations. External stakeholders include customers, payment providers, shipping providers, and suppliers. They exchange defined information with the system or are directly affected by its service quality. The customer is the central external stakeholder because the e-commerce system must support the full customer journey while preservingthe operational controls needed by internal staff.

1.4 Current Problems

1.4.1 Problem Identification
The company currently operates with information distributed across spreadsheets, staff messages, and separate partner portals. Product records can be edited by several people, inventory changes may be recorded after the physical movement occurs, and staff must often reconcile order, payment, and shipping information manually.
The main problems identified are:
1. Product and category information can be inconsistent across channels because catalogue records are edited in several locations.
2. Inventory availability may be inaccurate when stock movements are recorded after physical changes.
3. Staff manually reconcile order, payment, and shipping information across separate systems and partner portals.
4. Customers cannot reliably view order and delivery status in one place and contact staff for updates.
5. Sales and inventory reports take time to compile from separate records, delaying management decisions.
6. Product discovery can be slow when product information and search data are not consistently maintained.
7. Customer-service staff lack a single operational view of customer, order, and support context across messages and system records.

4. Provide customers and staff with current order and shipment status, including tracking information.
5. Support customer profiles, reviews, promotions, and staff service using shared operational information.
6. Give managers timely sales, order, inventory, and operating reports for business decisions.
 
Chapter 2 Requirements Analysis
2.1 Stakeholder Needs
This section translates the Hoodoo business context, stakeholders, information assets, problems, and objectives in Chapter 1 into outcomes expected from the system. The needs are expressed independently of screen layout or implementation technology. They provide the rationale for the requirements in Section 2.2 and for the use cases later in this chapter. Several needs cross organizational boundaries: accurate inventory supports customers, warehouse staff, and managers, while a coherent order record supports customers, sales staff, and external service coordination.

2.1.1 Stakeholder Objectives
Customers need to discover suitable products, compare current descriptions and prices, understand whether an item appears available, and complete an order with confidence. A visitor should be able to browse and search without providing unnecessary personal information. A registered customer needs an account view for personal order history and delivery status. After placing an order, the customer needs a clear distinction between order acceptance, payment processing, fulfilment, and shipment; these are different business events and must not be collapsed into one ambiguous “complete” state.

Administrators need to maintain product, category, price, attribute, and promotion data from a controlled source. They need validation that prevents incomplete or inconsistent information from being published. The system should make the current active record available consistently to public product views and internal staff views, while retaining an attributable history of significant updates.

Sales staff need to respond to questions using the order and customer context required for service. They need to identify the current state, see relevant status history, and record unresolved issues for follow-up. The system should reduce manual reconciliation and make it clear whether a case can be resolved by Hoodoo or needs an external payment or shipping provider.

Warehouse staff need to receive, adjust, pick, pack, and dispatch stock using product and quantity information that can be trusted. Their system actions must correspond to physical or approved operational events. They need a traceable record for receipts, reservations, fulfilment, and corrections so that stock discrepancies can be investigated rather than hidden by overwriting a balance.

Managers need timely views of sales, inventory, order, promotion, and service activity. They need to know the selected period and status definitions behind a report so they can interpret results consistently. They need to identify exceptions and trends without manually combining disconnected records.

The payment provider needs to receive an identifiable payment request and return an outcome that Hoodoo can link to the correct order. The provider remains responsible for payment processing. Hoodoo must not treat a request as paid until a valid result is received and associated with its transaction reference.

The shipping provider needs accurate delivery information for an eligible order and a way to return a shipment reference and tracking events. The provider remains responsible for physical carriage. Hoodoo needs to associate each returned event with the corresponding order and communicate only the latest status actually known.

Suppliers need a clear replenishment or receipt exchange where Hoodoo supports that workflow. If a direct system integration is unavailable, staff still need to record the source, product, and quantity of a receipt. Supplier records must remain distinguishable from customer orders.

The needs are related but not interchangeable. SN01 depends on the quality of catalogue attributes covered by SN02; searching incomplete or contradictory product data will not produce dependable discovery. SN03 depends on current prices, cart state, customer-supplied delivery details, inventory validation, and an external payment result. A successful button action is not sufficient proof of a successful order. The system must preserve the confirmed order information and show whether payment is accepted, declined, or still pending.

SN04 requires Hoodoo to distinguish physical on-hand stock from quantities that can be promised to a new order. A reservation changes availability; it does not represent a physical receipt. A receipt increases recorded on-hand stock, while fulfilment, cancellation, return, or correction follows an explicitly defined movement rule. Customer-facing availability and management reports should use the same definitions.

SN05 and SN07 describe separate states. An order can exist before a shipment is created. Payment acceptance does not mean an order has shipped, and a shipment label does not necessarily mean that a carrier has physically received the parcel. The customer view must show the latest status that the system has actually received and its update time when data may be delayed.

SN08 and SN10 constrain staff and partner access. Sales staff need relevant order context, but should not receive unrestricted access to customer or payment data. Payment credentials remain with the provider; Hoodoo retains the transaction reference and outcome needed for order operations. Significant changes and provider results should have an identifiable source and time where available.

SN09 depends on consistent source records and metric definitions. Sales reports need to state whether they count placed, paid, cancelled, refunded, or completed orders. Inventory reports need to distinguish on-hand, reserved, and available stock. Without those definitions, an apparently precise report can still lead to a poor decision.

FR02 describes the administrator’s maintenance service, while FR15 adds publication controls so the same active record is used across customer and staff views.

FR03 and FR04 support discovery, while FR05 presents the product detail that informs a purchase decision. FR07 defines access to a customer’s own order view, FR14 requires the underlying status history and notifications, and FR10 adds carrier information after a shipment exists. FR19 and FR20 support warehouse work while FR09 links stock changes to order activity. FR13 depends on these consistent operational records, and FR24 makes significant changes reviewable.

2.2.2 Non-functional Requirements
The following are proposed acceptance targets, not claims about an existing system. The project team should confirm workload assumptions, provider dependencies, and operational capacity during design.
Table 2.4 Non-functional requirements
 
ID
Non-functional requirement
NFR01
Response time — Under an agreed normal workload, at least 95% of catalogue browse, search, product-detail, and cart-view requests should complete within 3 seconds, excluding external-provider wait time.
NFR02
Checkout feedback — Checkout should show an accepted, pending, or rejected outcome within 5
seconds when the provider responds within its agreed service window; a slower response must remain pending without creating a duplicate order.
NFR03

NFR11

NFR12

NFR13

These values are stated now so quality can be evaluated in design and acceptance, not to imply that an operational service level has already been measured.

2.2.3 Requirement Prioritization
The MoSCoW method indicates the relative priority for the initial Hoodoo scope. “Must”
requirements are needed for a coherent shopping, order, inventory, and fulfilment flow. “Should”
requirements add important operating value and may be staged only if the initial release is constrained. “Could” requirements are useful extensions. “Won’t for this release” marks work outside the proposed baseline.
Table 2.5 MoSCoW priority summary
 
Priority
Requirements / scope
Must have
FR01–FR15, FR17, FR19–FR22; NFR01, NFR02, NFR04–NFR06, NFR08, NFR09, NFR15.
Should have
FR16, FR18, FR23–FR25; NFR03, NFR07, NFR10, NFR11, NFR13, NFR14.
Could have
Enhanced report exports, saved searches, configurable notification preferences, recommendation features, and additional supplier self-service where stakeholder value is confirmed.
Won’t for this release
Direct control of provider settlement, carrier route planning, supplier production planning, and automated refund or stock write-off decisions without authorized review.
 
Priorities should be confirmed with stakeholders before implementation. A “Must” label does not remove dependencies: payment handling relies on a provider interface, and tracking relies on provider data or an explicit staff-mediated process. If an integration is not available, the system should record the limitation and retain a reviewable state rather than imply that an external action has completed.

3.2 AI Requirement Chain
The requirement chain is: Chapter 1 problem P06, slow product discovery → SN01 product discovery and SN02 trustworthy information → FR03–FR05 and FR15 → UC13 Browse Products, UC02 Search Products, UC03 View Product Details, and search refinements UC20–UC22. UC07 Manage Catalogue supplies validated data under FR02 and FR15. AI may estimate relevance but cannot publish products or invent missing attributes. For example, “blue 0.5 mm pen, box of 12” refers to ink colour, tip size, and pack quantity; visually similar pens are not necessarily interchangeable.
Evaluate keyword, voice, and image search separately on a versioned stationery catalogue with reviewed relevance labels. Include synonyms, Vietnamese queries, exact specifications, ambiguous images, and no-match cases. Compare AI-assisted retrieval with a keyword baseline on the same data using suitable defined measures such as Precision@k, Recall@k, MRR, or nDCG@k. State k, language, catalogue snapshot, model/index version, workload, and label coverage. Assess speech-transcription errors together with final search relevance; assess image results for specification and selling-unit mismatches. NFR01 proposes a 95th-percentile response target of 3 seconds under agreed workload, excluding external-provider wait time; report end-to-end voice/image latency separately. Numerical improvements and acceptance thresholds require experiments and agreed criteria.
3.3 AI Architecture and Data Governance
The application accepts a keyword query, permitted voice input, or a validated image. Voice input is transcribed and confirmed or corrected by the customer before retrieval. Images are checked for supported type, size, and readability before processing. Text or image retrieval returns candidate product_id values; optional ranking orders them; the catalogue service removes unpublished or archived products and supplies current descriptions, prices, selling units, and availability. The index is not the stock authority. Checkout performs the final price and stock validation.
Each separately sold colour, size, or pack configuration is a product record with its own product_id, price, selling unit, and stock quantity. Search, product details, cart lines, and order lines preserve that identifier. Repeated additions of the same product_id increase its existing cart line; different identifiers remain separate. Individual items and packs have separate stock, with no automatic conversion. AI must not merge records merely because their names or images are similar.
Search and recognition do not create orders, reserve stock, change prices, or process payments. UC05 validates checkout, creates a unique pending-payment order, and reserves stock according to policy before UC19 sends a correlated payment request. Verified payment outcomes update the same order. AI output cannot mark an order paid, authorize customer-account changes, or bypass role permissions.
Validate product descriptions and image-use permissions before indexing. UC07 catalogue changes trigger index refresh; publication and archive rules must still hold when the index is stale. Record source data, catalogue snapshot, model/index version, evaluation configuration, and rollback procedure. Monitor delayed indexing and processing failures. Keep payment credentials and private order data out of search inputs and logs.
Public browsing and searching do not require an account. Microphone access requires permission. Define access controls, purpose, upload limits, retention, and deletion for recordings, images, transcripts, and query logs; minimize personal data and govern external speech/image services consistently with NFR05 and NFR13. Personal account/order access remains controlled by FR01, FR07, and NFR04; administrator actions require the corresponding role permissions.
3.4 Quality, Risk, and Human Control
Key risks include incomplete descriptions, poor images, speech-recognition errors, stale indexes, and confusion between similar stationery sold in different units. Validate explicit requested specifications against catalogue attributes where available. Do not infer missing ink colour, paper weight, page count, or pack quantity as fact. Image similarity does not guarantee an exact product or compatibility. Current prices and availability must come from authoritative records.
For microphone denial, failed transcription, invalid images, no matches, or unavailable models/indexes, display actionable feedback and offer retry, keyword search, or category browsing where available. An AI outage must not block independent account, cart, checkout, or personal-order functions. Administrator review controls publication and customer administration. Recommendations remain a Could-have option; demand estimation is future work without a dedicated baseline use case. Both require explicit ownership, requirements, evaluation, and human review before adoption. Test relevance, input failures, stale publication data, selling-unit mismatches, unauthorized disclosure, and unchanged transactional records after search. This chapter defines intended behaviour and evaluation plans, not completed implementation or measured accuracy.

4.2.1 Record and relationship mapping
One-to-many associations can be mapped using a reference on the many side. Category.parentId represents the optional parent-category association. ProductImage belongs to Product; CartItem belongs to Cart and references Product; OrderItem belongs to Order and references Product. SearchFilter and SearchResult belong to SearchQuery. Customer links identify the owner of a cart or order and the author of a review. Do not infer additional product variants, stock-conversion records, or payment-attempt entities from this diagram.
Product.stockQuantity is on-hand stock measured in sellingUnit; reservedQuantity is the portion reserved for orders. Available quantity is derived as stockQuantity minus reservedQuantity and need not be stored separately. For a box of 12 pens, sellingUnit is “box” and packQuantity is 12; stockQuantity counts boxes. PackQuantity describes contents and does not convert stock between this product and a separately sold loose pen. Price and line unitPrice are integer VND amounts in the current long-based model.
4.2.2 Integrity rules
• Each Product belongs to one Category. A Category may contain zero or more Products and have at most one parent; prevent cycles in the category hierarchy.
• Each CartItem refers to exactly one Product. A Cart may be empty. Quantities are positive whole numbers in the selling unit; one cart has at most one line for a given productId, and repeated additions increase that line.
• Each Order contains at least one OrderItem, with a positive whole-number quantity and the confirmed unitPrice. Retain the purchased terms when product prices later change; product-name, selling-unit, and delivery snapshots are future data-design details, not attributes already present in this VPP.
• An Order may exist with no Payment record; this supports creating the order before sending a payment request. The supplied multiplicity permits at most one Payment per Order, interpreted here as a current payment record. Pending, failed, and successful outcomes remain distinct. Retaining multiple payment attempts or individual callbacks requires additional design; it is not represented by the current diagram.
• Require 0 ≤ reservedQuantity ≤ stockQuantity and packQuantity ≥ 1. The cart does not reserve stock. Checkout validates availability and records the order/reservation before requesting payment; reservation release follows the agreed policy. Stock-movement history is outside this core diagram even though the wider requirements require it.
• Public search and visitor carts are permitted in Chapter 2, while this VPP requires one Customer for each SearchQuery and Cart. These multiplicities describe customer-linked records only and do not fully model anonymous sessions. Session ownership and optional customer links must be resolved in later design.
• SearchModality uses KEYWORD, VOICE, and IMAGE. Raw text may be absent for image input; storage of image/voice input and a Product reference for each SearchResult remain design details not fully captured by the current attributes and associations.
• Review belongs to one Customer and one Product. Rating bounds and purchase/delivery eligibility follow the Chapter 2 review policy; the diagram has no OrderItem-to-Review association that proves a verified purchase.
• Customer account status, administrator permissions, promotions, shipping/tracking, and order/stock histories are outside this diagram’s current scope. Their omission does not remove the corresponding Chapter 1–2 requirements.

SearchQuery, SearchFilter, SearchResult, QueryMedia; search_query, search_filter, search_result, query_media
Design adds encoderVersion, media references/expiry, explicit/hard/relaxed-filter flags, and relevance/preference/final scores with explanation. SearchResult references Product. SearchModality, MediaType, and FilterType provide typed choices; filter relaxation and preference scoring are present design mechanisms, not proof of approved personalization requirements.
Shopping

Cart has identity, status, and timestamps; cart_item adds cart_item_id and references cart_id and product_id, with quantity and unit_price. The physical cart requires customer_id. There is no ProductSize table in the physical model.
Orders and payments
Order, OrderItem, Payment; customer_order, order_item, payment
Physical customer_order stores address, subtotal, shipping_fee, total_amount, status, and timestamps. OrderItem stores product_name_snapshot. Payment stores method, amount, status, transaction_code, idempotency_key, failure_reason, and timestamps; order_id is unique.
Feedback

Review records customer/product references, rating, comment, and timestamps. The physical rating constraint is 1–5. No direct OrderItem-to-Review relationship is defined.
4.3.2 Design boundaries
The new physical Category table has category_id, name, slug, level, sort_order, is_active, and nullable parent_id. Product.category_id references this Category; parent_id references Category.category_id. The project also retains an older lowercase category table and an empty Entity table; they must not be mistaken for additional business concepts required by the analysis model.
Physical product.price is present and NOT NULL. stockQuantity, reservedQuantity, sellingUnit, and packQuantity are also NOT NULL. The status column is still typed BOOLEAN with default FALSE in this VPP, despite its new name; it therefore represents a two-state flag, not the analysis model’s unrestricted String status. The file’s sales_count and average_rating remain stored aggregate fields. Their presence does not establish an update policy. The SQL money fields use integer/decimal types while analysis uses long; the currency/scale mapping must be agreed explicitly.
The physical payment.order_id is unique and NOT NULL, allowing zero or one payment record per order; the order can be inserted before payment. search_query.customer_id is nullable, supporting anonymous search. cart.customer_id remains mandatory, so visitor-cart session ownership is not fully represented. QueryMedia and SearchResult-to-Product are refinements absent from the analysis file. Physical customer_order includes a shipping address, but recipient/contact snapshots and full status histories are not defined.
The class views still differ from this ERD: Category has categoryId/name/description, Product retains material/gender/published and does not carry the physical stock fields, and ProductSize-related classes remain. These are recorded as source-file differences; this chapter does not silently claim that the VPP itself has been corrected. Legacy SearchService.filterByAvailableSizes(), filter relaxation, and preference scoring also require reconciliation with the current product_id-based requirements before code generation.

Chapter 5 Code
5.1 Implementation Structure
A possible implementation groups modules by business capability: identity, catalogue, cart, orders, inventory, payments, fulfilment and reporting. Each module exposes a service interface and owns its validation rules. Repository interfaces isolate persistence, and adapters encapsulate external payment, delivery and search services. This chapter is an implementation proposal, not a claim that an executable system or tests have been completed.
5.2 Core Order Algorithm
The pseudocode below illustrates the intended controls. Transaction boundaries, reservation expiry and failure handling require a concrete database and approved business policies.
placeOrder(customer, cart, delivery, requestKey):
require authenticated customer and ownership of cart if an order exists for requestKey: return that order validate delivery and nonempty cart
begin transaction
lock inventory records in a stable order
validate publication, current prices and available quantities calculate total using approved delivery and promotion rules
create pending order with item and price snapshots
reserve inventory; record requestKey and payment event commit transaction
return pending order
 
handlePaymentCallback(event):
verify provider signature and identify payment attempt if event was already processed: return success
begin transaction
record event and update payment state once
update order state according to verified outcome enqueue fulfilment or approved failure handling
commit transaction
The outgoing event should be recorded atomically with the order change and delivered by a worker with retries. A provider call should not be held inside a long database transaction. Concurrent tests must establish that stock cannot be oversold and repeated requests cannot create duplicate orders.

5.4 Test Strategy
Unit tests should cover totals, eligibility, state transitions and authorization. Integration tests should exercise database transactions, callback verification, duplicate events and timeouts. Concurrent checkout tests should use limited stock and multiple requests. End-to-end scenarios should cover
successful checkout, declined and pending payment, unavailable stock, catalogue publication, shipment updates and reporting. Each case should state starting data, action, expected visible response and persisted records.
Security tests should include cross-customer order access, unauthorized staff operations, malformed

input and replayed callbacks. Recovery tests should restore backups and reconcile outstanding payment events. Performance and availability targets must be agreed and measured under a documented workload; no results are inferred from the design.
5.5 Deployment and Evolution
Deploy the transactional application, database, background workers and integration adapters with separated configuration and secrets. Apply versioned migrations, monitor errors and queue delays, and maintain tested backups and rollback procedures. Introduce search and AI components behind stable interfaces, evaluate them with representative data and retain a fallback. Release planning should resolve guest checkout, reservation expiry, refunds, retention and provider retry limits with accountable
business owners.

Conclusion
The proposed Hoodoo information system connects product discovery, ordering, payment, inventory, fulfilment and reporting through defined responsibilities and information flows. The analysis identifies stakeholder needs and validation issues; the design describes how those needs may be implemented
while preserving distinct transactional states. AI can improve discovery and support decisions when evaluated against documented examples and governed as a replaceable service. Outstanding business policies and implementation evidence must be established before production deployment.
